# Lesson 35 storyboard — Homeostasis (folder lesson-30, v1)

First lesson of chapter B5, Homeostasis and response.

Big idea: your body keeps conditions such as temperature, blood glucose and water at the right level, using automatic control systems made of receptors, coordination centres and effectors, which bring any level that drifts too high or too low back to the optimum.

Flow note: the lesson follows one body through changing conditions. It opens with something already known from the enzymes lesson: what heat does to an enzyme. The lesson then answers "why keep things steady, and how?" in three steps.
1. **Why keep things steady?** Three levels on gauges (temperature, glucose, water), why cells and enzymes need them right, the word homeostasis, and that control systems are automatic and use nerves or hormones.
2. **What does the work?** A person steps into a cold wind. The stimulus, receptors in the skin, coordination centres (brain, spinal cord, pancreas) and effectors (a muscle), shown one at a time on one body, then together.
3. **Too high or too low?** A level gauge beside the three parts: the optimum, too high → decreased, too low → increased, then the whole loop.

Why this order: the reason (enzymes need the right conditions) comes before the name, so homeostasis has a purpose. The three parts come before the loop because "too high → decrease" only makes sense once learners know which part detects and which part responds. Nerves and hormones are only named here; the next lessons teach them.

1. Start here (B30-01): what happens to an enzyme heated far above body temperature (links to enzymes).
2. Why keep things steady? (B30-02–04): levels kept steady → why it matters → homeostasis → control systems. Checks: what homeostasis is; why temperature is kept near 37 °C.
3. What does the work? (B30-05–07): stimulus → receptors → coordination centres → effectors → put together. Checks: a numbered body diagram (which is a coordination centre); what an effector does.
4. Too high or too low? (B30-08–10): optimum → too high → too low → put together. Checks: blood glucose too high; what the optimum is.
5. On your own (B30-11–15): a hot car (which is the stimulus); why control systems are automatic; a numbered loop diagram in a new order (which part produces the response); an invented temperature graph read without over-claiming; teacher-reviewed written answer.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; British spelling; other lessons referred to by topic only.

Out of scope: how temperature is controlled in detail (sweating, shivering, blood vessels) and the thermoregulatory centre, which are separate Biology only; how blood glucose is controlled by insulin (a later lesson); the word "negative feedback"; the nervous system in detail (next lesson); hormones in detail (a later lesson); water balance and the kidney.

## States in full

### B30-01 · choice · `priorKnowledge`, `diagnostic`
**Q:** An enzyme is heated far above body temperature. What happens to it?
- 0 It works faster and faster · **1 Its shape changes and it stops working ✓** · 2 It turns into glucose · 3 Nothing happens to it
- Hint: What does too much heat do to an enzyme’s active site?
- Explanation: High temperatures change the shape of an enzyme’s active site. So the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme stops working.

### B30-02 · teach "Why keep things steady?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kept steady | Some conditions inside you stay at the right level. | temperature, glucose, water | Your body keeps some conditions inside it steady. Your body temperature stays close to 37 °C. The amount of glucose in your blood and the amount of water in your body are kept steady too. | `nerve-home-levels` |
| Why it matters | Cells and enzymes need the right conditions. | right conditions → enzymes work | Your cells need the right conditions to work properly. This includes the right conditions for their enzymes. You met enzymes when you learned how food is digested. If you got much too hot, your enzymes would change shape and stop working. | `nerve-home-enzyme` |
| Homeostasis | Homeostasis keeps conditions at the right level as things change. | changes happen → levels kept right | Conditions keep changing, outside and inside your body. A hot day is a change outside. Muscles working hard are a change inside. Keeping the conditions in your body and cells at the right level, as these changes happen, is called homeostasis. | `nerve-home-changes` |
| Control systems | Automatic control systems do the work. | automatic: nerves or hormones | Your body keeps conditions steady using control systems. They are automatic, so you do not have to think about them. They work using your nerves or chemicals called hormones. You will learn about nerves in the next lesson. | `nerve-home-control` |

### B30-03 · choice
**Q:** What is homeostasis?
- **0 Keeping conditions in the body and cells at the right level ✓** · 1 Making the body as warm as possible · 2 Stopping all changes outside the body · 3 Breaking down food with enzymes
- Hint: Think about the word “steady”.
- Explanation: Conditions inside and outside the body keep changing. Homeostasis keeps conditions in the body and cells at the right level as these changes happen.

### B30-04 · choice
**Q:** Why does the body keep its temperature close to 37 °C?
- 0 So the body never uses any energy · 1 So the blood stops moving · **2 So cells and their enzymes can work properly ✓**
- Hint: What happens to enzymes if conditions are wrong?
- Explanation: Cells and their enzymes need the right conditions. So keeping the temperature steady lets them work properly.

### B30-05 · teach "What does the work?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A change | A change in the environment is a stimulus. | stimulus = a change | You step out of a warm house into a cold wind. The temperature around you drops. A change in the environment like this is called a stimulus. Two or more of them are called stimuli. | `nerve-parts-stimulus` |
| Receptors | Receptors detect a stimulus. | receptor = detects | Cells in your skin detect that the air has got colder. Cells that detect a stimulus are called receptors. | `nerve-parts-receptor` |
| Coordination centres | A coordination centre decides what to do. | coordination centre = processes, then organises | The receptors send information to a coordination centre. It receives and processes the information, then organises a response. The brain, the spinal cord and the pancreas are all coordination centres. | `nerve-parts-centre` |
| Effectors | Effectors carry out the response. | effector = responds | The coordination centre sends information on to effectors. Effectors are the parts that produce a response, such as muscles. Here, the response helps bring your body temperature back up. | `nerve-parts-effector` |
| Put it together | Receptor → coordination centre → effector. | detect → decide → do | Every control system has these three parts. Receptors detect the stimulus. The coordination centre organises a response. Effectors carry out the response. | `nerve-parts-all` |

### B30-06 · choice · question diagram `nerve-parts-question` (assessment version hides the answer)
**Q:** Look at the numbered parts. Which number shows a coordination centre?
- 0 Number 1 · **1 Number 2 ✓** · 2 Number 3
- Hint: Which part receives and processes the information?
- Explanation: Number 1 is a receptor in the skin, and number 3 is a muscle, an effector. Number 2 is the brain, which is a coordination centre.

### B30-07 · choice
**Q:** What is the job of an effector?
- 0 To detect a stimulus · 1 To process information · **2 To produce a response ✓** · 3 To store glucose
- Hint: Effectors come last in a control system.
- Explanation: Receptors detect a stimulus, and coordination centres process the information. Effectors produce the response.

### B30-08 · teach "Too high or too low?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| The optimum | Each condition has an ideal level. | optimum = the ideal level | For each condition, there is a best level for your cells. This ideal level is called the optimum. The real level drifts a little above and below it. | `nerve-loop-optimum` |
| Too high | If the level is too high, it is brought down. | too high → decrease | Say the level rises too high. Receptors detect the change, and the coordination centre organises a response. Effectors then decrease the level, back towards the optimum. | `nerve-loop-high` |
| Too low | If the level is too low, it is brought up. | too low → increase | If the level falls too low, the same three parts work the other way. Receptors detect the fall. Effectors then increase the level, back towards the optimum. | `nerve-loop-low` |
| Put it together | The level is kept close to the optimum. | rise → brought down; fall → brought up | The control system keeps working all the time. Each time the level moves away from the optimum, it is brought back. So the level always stays close to the optimum. | `nerve-loop-all` |

### B30-09 · choice
**Q:** The amount of glucose in someone’s blood rises too high. What does their control system do?
- 0 Increases it even more · 1 Waits until it stops rising by itself · 2 Removes all the glucose from the blood · **3 Decreases it back towards the optimum ✓**
- Hint: Too high: which way must the level go?
- Explanation: If a level is too high, effectors act to decrease it. So the level falls back towards the optimum.

### B30-10 · choice
**Q:** What is the optimum level?
- **0 The ideal level for the body’s cells ✓** · 1 The highest level possible · 2 The lowest level possible
- Hint: Think about the level the control system keeps bringing things back to.
- Explanation: The control system brings a level back whenever it rises or falls. It brings it back to the optimum, the ideal level for the cells.

### B30-11 · choice · `application`, independent
**Q:** Someone sits in a hot car on a sunny day. Their body temperature starts to rise. What is the stimulus?
- **0 The rise in temperature ✓** · 1 Their skin · 2 Their brain · 3 Their muscles
- Hint: A stimulus is a change, not a body part.
- Explanation: The skin contains receptors, the brain is a coordination centre and muscles are effectors. The stimulus is the change: the rise in temperature.

### B30-12 · choice · `understanding`, independent
**Q:** Why are the body’s control systems called automatic?
- 0 They only work while you sleep · 1 You have to decide to switch them on · 2 They are controlled by machines · **3 They work without you having to think about them ✓**
- Hint: Do you choose to keep your temperature steady?
- Explanation: You do not decide to keep your temperature or water level steady. Control systems work without you having to think about them, so they are automatic.

### B30-13 · choice · `understanding`, independent · question diagram `nerve-loop-question` (assessment version hides the answer)
**Q:** The diagram shows a control system. Which number is the part that produces the response?
- 0 Number 1 · **1 Number 2 ✓** · 2 Number 3
- Hint: Follow the arrows from the level. Which part acts last?
- Explanation: The arrows go from the level to part 3, which detects the change, then to part 1, the coordination centre. Part 2 comes last and brings the level back, so it is the effector.

### B30-14 · choice · `dataInterpretation`, independent · question diagram `nerve-temp-data` (assessment version hides the answer)
**Q:** The graph shows one person’s body temperature during a day. Which conclusion fits it?
- 0 Their temperature stayed exactly the same all day · 1 Everyone’s temperature is always 37.0 °C · **2 Their temperature rose and fell a little but stayed close to 37 °C ✓** · 3 Their control system stopped working at midday
- Hint: Look at how far the line moves away from 37 °C.
- Explanation: The readings went from 36.7 °C to 37.3 °C; one person cannot show what everyone’s temperature does. So their temperature rose and fell a little but stayed close to 37 °C.

### B30-15 · written · `teacherOnly`
**Q:** Your body temperature starts to rise while you run on a hot day. Describe how a control system brings it back to normal. Use the words receptor, coordination centre and effector.
- Hint: Take the three parts in order, then say which way the temperature changes.
- Model answer: The rise in temperature is a stimulus. Receptors detect the rise and send information to a coordination centre, such as the brain. The coordination centre processes the information and organises a response. Effectors produce the response, which decreases the temperature back towards the optimum.
- Marking points: Receptors detect the rise in temperature, the stimulus. · Information goes to a coordination centre, such as the brain. · The coordination centre processes the information and organises a response. · Effectors produce a response that decreases the temperature back to the optimum.
- Common errors: Saying effectors detect the change, or that receptors produce the response. · Saying the level is increased when it is too high. · Saying you have to decide to cool down; the control system is automatic.
