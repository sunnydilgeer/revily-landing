# Lesson 25 storyboard — Testing new drugs (v2)

Big idea: before doctors can use a new drug, it is tested in stages — on cells and animals, then on people — to find out whether it works, how harmful it is and the best dose, and the results are checked by other scientists before they are published.

Flow note: the lesson follows one possible new drug, drug M, from a lab bench to a published report. It opens where Lesson 24 ended: chemists find a plant chemical that might ease migraines. Drug M then goes through each stage in order: human cells and tissues, live animals, healthy volunteers on a very low dose, and then patients, including Mia's aunt. Next come the fair-test ideas (placebo, blind, double-blind), and the lesson ends with peer review. The order matters because each stage only happens if the one before it went well. Harm is checked on cells and animals before any person takes the drug, and on healthy people before ill people. The fair-test ideas come after the patients, because they are about comparing patients. Peer review comes last, because it checks results that have already been gathered.

1. Start here (B25-01): which plant was aspirin first made from? (links to Lesson 24).
2. Testing in the lab (B25-02–04): a possible new drug → preclinical testing on cells and tissues → live animals → efficacy → toxicity → dosage. Checks: what is tested first; which property a harmful result describes.
3. Clinical trials (B25-05–07): a clinical trial → healthy volunteers first → a very low dose → then patients → the optimum dose. Checks: why healthy volunteers come first; a numbered stages diagram (when people first take the drug).
4. A fair test (B25-08–10): two groups → placebo → blind → double-blind → peer review. Checks: what a placebo is; who knows in a double-blind trial.
5. On your own (B25-11–15): an original dose chart (the optimum dose); why cells and animals come first; why double-blind; an original trial-results chart read without over-claiming; teacher-reviewed written description of the stages.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; words written out in full; British spelling. Neutral, factual tone about animal testing and drug companies: describe what happens, with no moralising either way. No absolute claims ("most effective", "few side effects", not "safe" or "no side effects").

Out of scope: the names of trial phases (phase 1, 2, 3); regulators and licensing; the cost and length of drug development; statistics and sample size; the ethics of animal testing as a debate; monoclonal antibodies (Biology only).

Source boundary: supplied page 43 (reference only); AQA 8464 section 4.3.1.9 (second part). Drug M, its story, the questions and both charts are original. Draft pending teacher review.

- Hint: Group 2 is compared with group 1.
- Explanation: A placebo looks like the drug, so the patients cannot tell them apart. But it does not do anything.

### B25-13 · choice · `understanding`, independent
**Q:** Why are many clinical trials double-blind?
- **0 So that nobody's expectations can affect the results ✓** · 1 So that the drug works faster · 2 So that there is no need for a placebo
- Hint: Knowing which pill someone got could change how they feel or judge the results.
- Explanation: If patients or doctors know who got drug M, their expectations could change the results. In a double-blind trial nobody knows, so nobody's expectations can affect the results.

**Q:** The chart shows migraine days in a double-blind trial. Which conclusion fits it?
- 0 Drug M cures migraines for everyone · 1 The placebo had no effect at all · **2 Both groups improved, but drug M reduced migraine days more ✓** · 3 Drug M made migraines worse
- Hint: Compare how far each group's bars fell.
- Explanation: Both groups had fewer migraine days, so the placebo group improved too, and nothing shows a cure for everyone. Drug M's group fell further, from 8 to 3 days, than the placebo group, from 8 to 6.

### B25-15 · written · `teacherOnly`
**Q:** Describe the stages a new drug goes through before doctors can use it.
- Hint: Start in the lab and end with the published results.
- Model answer: First the drug is tested on human cells and tissues in the lab, then on live animals, to find its efficacy, toxicity and dosage. If it passes, it is tested in a clinical trial: first on healthy volunteers at a very low dose, then on patients, to find the optimum dose. Patients may be given the drug or a placebo in a double-blind trial. The results are checked by peer review before they are published.
- Marking points: Preclinical testing: cells and tissues, then live animals. · Efficacy, toxicity and dosage are checked. · Clinical trial: healthy volunteers on a very low dose, then patients. · Comparison with a placebo, often double-blind. · Peer review before the results are published.
- Common errors: Putting people before animals, or patients before healthy volunteers. · Saying a placebo is a weaker dose of the drug. · Mixing up blind and double-blind. · Saying peer review is done by the company that made the drug.
