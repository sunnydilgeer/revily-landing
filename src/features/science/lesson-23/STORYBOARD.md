# Lesson 23 storyboard — Vaccination (v2)

Big idea: a vaccine gives your white blood cells a safe practice run, so they can respond fast if the real pathogen arrives; when most people are vaccinated, the pathogen struggles to spread to anyone.

Flow note: the lesson follows one pupil, Sam, and his class. It opens with something familiar: chickenpox goes round the class, but Sam stays well because he had it before. That story teaches the first (slow) and second (fast) response. Then Sam turns out to be immune to measles too, without ever having had it, because of a vaccine. The vaccine is taught as the same fast response without the illness. Finally measles arrives at his school, and the lesson widens to the whole school, then to the limits of vaccines. Natural immunity comes before vaccination because a vaccine only makes sense as "the second time, without the first illness". The individual comes before the population because protecting others depends on each person being immune.

1. Start here (B23-01): what do antibodies lock onto? (links to Lesson 22).
2. Why Sam does not catch it twice (B23-02–04): chickenpox in class → first time, slow → second time, fast → immune → the response on a graph. Checks: why Sam stayed well; numbered antibody-level graph (labels hidden).
3. A safe practice run (B23-05–07): immune to measles without having had it → vaccination → dead or inactive → antibodies without illness → the real pathogen later. Checks: what a vaccine contains; numbered four-stage vaccine diagram (captions hidden).
4. Protecting everyone (B23-08–10): measles arrives at school → nowhere to spread → protecting pupils who are not vaccinated → epidemics → not perfect (pros and cons summary). Checks: numbered crowd diagram (why person 1 is safe); a disadvantage of vaccination.
5. On your own (B23-11–15): vaccination rate falls in a town; why inactive pathogens still trigger antibodies; why a measles vaccine does not protect against chickenpox; an original antibody-level graph read without over-claiming; teacher-reviewed written explanation.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; words written out in full; British spelling. Neutral, factual tone on vaccination: state benefits and limits side by side, with no moralising and no absolute claims ("less likely", "usually", not "never" or "always").

Out of scope: "herd immunity" as a required term (the idea is described, not named); vaccination schedules; named side effects beyond a general mild reaction; memory cells and other white blood cell types; how vaccines are made; antibiotics and painkillers (Lesson 24); testing new drugs (Lesson 25).

Source boundary: supplied pages 36 and 40–41 (reference only); AQA 8464 section 4.3.1.7. All scenarios, questions, the pupil story and the graphs are original. Draft pending teacher review.

### B23-06 · choice
**Q:** What does a vaccine put into the body?
- 0 Antibodies against the pathogen · 1 Lots of live pathogens · **2 Small amounts of dead or inactive pathogen ✓** · 3 Antitoxins
- Hint: It must be safe, but still look like the pathogen to white blood cells.

### B23-09 · choice · question diagram `vaccine-school-question` (assessment version hides the answer)
Diagram: a crowd of pupils; the one marked 1 has no shield, and everyone around them has one. A pathogen arrow from an ill visitor stops at the shielded pupils. No answer text appears in the diagram or its title.

**Q:** Person 1 is not vaccinated, but everyone around them is. Why is person 1 unlikely to catch measles?
- 0 Person 1 is already immune · 1 Measles cannot infect people who are not vaccinated · 2 The vaccinated people make antibodies for person 1 · **3 Few people near them can catch measles and pass it on ✓**
- Hint: Follow the path the virus would need to take to reach person 1.
- Explanation: The people around person 1 are immune, so they do not catch measles. So there are few people who could pass the virus on to person 1.

### B23-10 · choice
**Q:** Which of these is a disadvantage of vaccination?
- **0 A vaccine does not always make a person immune ✓** · 1 A vaccine makes the pathogen multiply · 2 A vaccine gives you the full disease
- Hint: Think about what happens in a small number of people.
- Explanation: Vaccines contain dead or inactive pathogens, so they cannot multiply or cause the disease. But vaccines do not always work, so a few people do not become immune.

### B23-11 · choice · `application`, independent
**Q:** In one town, far fewer children are now vaccinated against measles. What is most likely to happen?
- 0 Vaccinated children lose their immunity · 1 The measles virus loses its antigens · 2 Measles disappears from the town · **3 Measles spreads more easily, and outbreaks become more likely ✓**
- Hint: How many people could now catch the virus and pass it on?
- Explanation: With fewer immune people, more people can catch measles and pass it on. So the virus spreads more easily, and an outbreak is more likely.

### B23-12 · choice · `understanding`, independent
**Q:** A vaccine contains inactive pathogens. Why do white blood cells still make antibodies against them?
- **0 The pathogens still carry their antigens ✓** · 1 The pathogens multiply quickly · 2 The pathogens make toxins
- Hint: What do antibodies lock onto?
- Explanation: Inactive pathogens cannot multiply or cause disease. They still carry their antigens, so white blood cells make antibodies against them.

### B23-13 · choice · `understanding`, independent
**Q:** A measles vaccine does not protect against chickenpox. Why not?
- 0 Chickenpox is caused by bacteria · **1 Measles antibodies do not fit chickenpox antigens ✓** · 2 The measles vaccine only lasts a week
- Hint: Think about how an antibody fits an antigen.
- Explanation: Measles and chickenpox are different viruses with different antigens. Each antibody fits only one type of antigen, so measles antibodies do not fit chickenpox antigens.

### B23-14 · choice · `dataInterpretation`, independent · question diagram `vaccine-response-data` (assessment version hides the answer)
Diagram: an original graph of one person's antibody level over 20 weeks. The vaccine is given at week 0 (a slow, low rise), and the real pathogen enters at week 12 (a fast, high rise). The axes are labelled, with no conclusion text.

**Q:** The graph shows one person's antibody level. Which conclusion fits it?
- 0 The vaccine caused more antibodies than the real pathogen did · 1 The vaccine will protect everyone who has it · **2 After the pathogen entered, antibodies rose faster and higher than after the vaccine ✓** · 3 The antibody level stayed the same all year
- Hint: Compare the two rises on the graph.
- Explanation: After the vaccine at week 0, the level rose slowly and stayed low. After the pathogen at week 12, it rose faster and higher. One person's graph cannot show that the vaccine works for everyone.

### B23-15 · written · `teacherOnly`
**Q:** Explain how a measles vaccine stops a person becoming ill if measles later gets into their body.
- Hint: Start with what is in the vaccine, then say what white blood cells do each time.
- Model answer: The vaccine contains small amounts of dead or inactive measles virus. These carry antigens, so white blood cells make antibodies against them. If the real virus gets in later, white blood cells recognise its antigens and quickly make lots of antibodies. The virus is destroyed before the person becomes ill.
- Marking points: The vaccine contains dead or inactive pathogen. · Its antigens cause white blood cells to make antibodies. · Later, the right antibodies are made quickly and in large amounts. · The pathogen is destroyed before the person becomes ill.
- Common errors: Saying the vaccine contains antibodies. · Mixing up antigens and antibodies. · Saying the vaccine contains live pathogens or gives a mild version of the disease. · Saying the person can never catch any disease.
