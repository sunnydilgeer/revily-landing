# Lesson 54 storyboard — Quadrats and transects practical

Fourth lesson of chapter B7, Ecology. Folder `lesson-49`, id `B-ECO-049-B`, skill `B-ECO-SAMPLING`. Required practical 7 (preparation only).

Big idea: you cannot count every organism in a field, so you sample: quadrats placed at random compare how common an organism is in two areas and let you estimate a population, and a transect shows how its distribution changes along a line.

Flow note: one school field with a big tree carries the lesson; clover is common in the sun and rare in the shade.
1. **How do you sample?** Distribution first (the thing being measured), then the quadrat, placing it at random with two tape measures, counting and repeating, and comparing two areas fairly. The method comes before the maths because the numbers only mean something once you know where they came from.
2. **Find the averages.** The same seven sunny-area counts give the mean, then the median and mode. A worked shady-area mean, then guided practice with new numbers.
3. **How many in the field?** Scaling up: area ÷ quadrat area, then × mean. Worked example, guided practice with new numbers, then an independent item in "On your own".
4. **Along a line.** Transects (counting what touches the tape, or quadrats every 2 m), then percentage cover for things too hard to count, with a worked 38% example.

Sections:
1. Start here (B49-01): why clover grows poorly under a tree (light intensity, from abiotic factors).
2. How do you sample? (B49-02–04): checks why quadrats are placed at random; why use many quadrats.
3. Find the averages (B49-05–08): worked mean (shady area, 2 per quadrat); guided mean (6); guided median (5).
4. How many in the field? (B49-09–11): worked estimate (4800 buttercups); guided estimate (6000).
5. Along a line (B49-12–14): checks a percentage cover (64%); when to use a transect.
6. On your own (B49-15–19): population estimate from raw counts (1000, calculation); percentage cover of a 25-square quadrat (36%, assessment view); a mode (2); an invented moss transect chart read without over-claiming; teacher-reviewed written method comparing a sunny and a shady area.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; abiotic factors referred to by topic; British spelling; units written as m and m². The lesson says it prepares for the required practical and never claims to complete it; the only safety note is washing hands after handling soil and plants.

Out of scope: capture–recapture; kite diagrams; systematic sampling beyond the transect; standard deviation; the book's daisy and tulip examples and numbers.

| Distribution | Distribution is how organisms are spread across an area. | where they are, and how many | Clover grows thickly in the sunny part of this field, but hardly at all in the shade of the tree. ### B49-06 · worked example "Find the mean in the shady area" · diagram `eco-mean-worked`
**Q:** In the shady area, 5 quadrats held 2, 0, 3, 1 and 4 clover plants. What is the mean number per quadrat?
1. Add up the counts: 2 + 0 + 3 + 1 + 4 = 10.
2. Count the quadrats: there are 5.
3. Divide the total by the number of quadrats: 10 ÷ 5 = 2.
4. The mean is 2 clover plants per quadrat, far fewer than 9 in the sunny area.

### B49-10 · worked example "Estimate a population" · diagram `eco-estimate-worked`
**Q:** A lawn has an area of 200 m². Quadrats of 0.25 m² found a mean of 6 buttercups per quadrat. Estimate the number of buttercups on the lawn.
1. Divide the area of the lawn by the area of one quadrat: 200 ÷ 0.25 = 800.
2. So 800 quadrats would fit on the lawn.
3. Multiply by the mean number per quadrat: 800 × 6 = 4800.
4. There are about 4800 buttercups on the lawn.

### B49-19 · written · `explanation, transfer`
**Q:** Describe how to use quadrats to compare the clover in a sunny area and a shady area of a school field.
- Hint: Think: where each quadrat goes, what you count, how many times, and what you work out at the end.
- Model answer: Use two tape measures and random numbers to place a quadrat at random in the sunny area. Count the clover plants inside it. Repeat many times, then work out the mean number per quadrat. Do the same in the shady area, using the same size and number of quadrats. Then compare the two means.
- Teacher-reviewed (not auto-marked). Rubric points:
  - Place a quadrat at random, for example using random coordinates along two tape measures.
  - Count the clover plants inside the quadrat.
  - Repeat many times, then work out the mean number per quadrat.
  - Do the same in the other area with the same size and number of quadrats, then compare the means.
- Reject:
  - Choosing spots that look full of clover.
  - Using only one quadrat in each area.
  - Using different sizes of quadrat in the two areas.
