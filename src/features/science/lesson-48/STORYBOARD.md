# Lesson 53 storyboard — Food chains and predator–prey cycles

Third lesson of chapter B7, Ecology. Folder `lesson-48`, id `B-ECO-048-B`, skill `B-ECO-FOOD-CHAINS`.

Big idea: every food chain starts with a producer that makes food using energy from the Sun; biomass passes along the chain to primary, secondary and tertiary consumers; and the numbers of predators and their prey rise and fall in linked cycles, with the predator lagging behind.

Flow note: one woodland food chain (oak leaves → caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk) is built from the producer upwards, then one rabbit-and-fox graph is walked through a full cycle.
1. **Where does food come from?** Producers first (photosynthesis, by topic), then biomass, then the food chain and its arrows. Arrow direction is taught before any naming, because every later question depends on it.
2. **Who eats whom?** Consumers, then primary, secondary and tertiary in turn on the same chain, then "count along the arrows from the producer".
3. **Predators and prey.** Predator and prey defined, then the cycle one step per frame (more rabbits → more foxes; more foxes → fewer rabbits; fewer rabbits → fewer foxes, so rabbits recover), then the lag and why it happens (foxes take time to breed).

Sections:
1. Start here (B48-01): where a plant gets energy to make food (links to photosynthesis).
2. Where does food come from? (B48-02–04): checks what algae are; what the arrow from caterpillar to blue tit shows.
3. Who eats whom? (B48-05–07): checks a numbered pond chain, which is the secondary consumer; how many consumers in grass → rabbit → fox.
4. Predators and prey (B48-08–10): checks why fox numbers rise later; what happens to rabbits when foxes peak.
5. On your own (B48-11–15): the tertiary consumer in a pond chain; which line on an unlabelled graph is the owl (predator) population; an invented vole and owl table read without over-claiming; a disease kills most rabbits; teacher-reviewed written answer on why the numbers cycle.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; photosynthesis and biotic factors referred to by topic; British spelling. Arrows always point to the eater. On every graph the predator line is lower and its peaks come after the prey peaks.

Out of scope: pyramids of biomass and the efficiency of biomass transfer (not on this page); decomposers; food webs (the communities lesson); the book's grass → grasshopper → rat → snake question.

Source boundary: supplied revision-guide page 82 (scope only); AQA 8464 section 4.7.2.1. Foxes and rabbits are the standard predator–prey example; the woodland and pond chains, vole and owl data, all questions and all wording are original. Draft pending teacher review.

The last frame shows all four tags.

## States in full

### B48-01 · choice · `understanding, priorKnowledge, diagnostic`
**Q:** Where does a plant get the energy to make its own food?
- 0 From the soil · **1 From light from the Sun ✓** · 2 From the water it takes in · 3 From animals that eat it
- Hint: You met photosynthesis when you learned how plants make glucose.
- Explanation: Soil and water give a plant mineral ions and water, not energy. Plants use energy from sunlight to make glucose by photosynthesis.

### B48-02 · teach "Where does food come from?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Producers | Producers make their own food using energy from the Sun. | Sun → producer | Green plants and algae make their own food. They use light from the Sun to make glucose by photosynthesis. An organism that makes its own food is called a producer. | `eco-chain-producer` |
| Biomass | Some glucose is used to build new living material. | glucose → new leaves, wood, roots | An oak tree uses some of its glucose to grow new leaves, wood and roots. The mass of living material in an organism is called its biomass. | `eco-chain-biomass` |
| Food chains | A food chain shows what is eaten by what. | arrow = is eaten by | Caterpillars eat oak leaves, blue tits eat caterpillars, and sparrowhawks eat blue tits. A diagram showing this is called a food chain. Each arrow points to the eater. Biomass passes along the chain as each organism is eaten. | `eco-chain-arrows` |

### B48-03 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** In a pond, algae make their own food using light. What are the algae?
- **0 Producers ✓** · 1 Consumers · 2 Predators
- Hint: Do they make their food or eat it?
- Explanation: Algae make their own food by photosynthesis. An organism that makes its own food is a producer.

### B48-04 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** In a food chain, an arrow goes from the caterpillar to the blue tit. What does it show?
- 0 The caterpillar eats the blue tit · 1 The blue tit makes food for the caterpillar · **2 The blue tit eats the caterpillar ✓**
- Hint: Which way does an arrow point?
- Explanation: Each arrow points to the eater. So the blue tit eats the caterpillar, and biomass passes from the caterpillar to the blue tit.

### B48-05 · teach "Who eats whom?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumers | Consumers eat other organisms. | consumer = eats others | Animals cannot make their own food. They get it by eating other organisms. Organisms that eat other organisms are called consumers. | `eco-chain-consumers` |
| Primary consumers | Primary consumers eat producers. | 1st consumer eats the producer | The first consumer in a food chain eats the producer. It is called the primary consumer. Here, the caterpillar is the primary consumer, because it eats oak leaves. | `eco-chain-primary` |
| Secondary consumers | Secondary consumers eat primary consumers. | 2nd consumer eats the 1st | The next consumer eats the primary consumer. It is called the secondary consumer. Here, the blue tit is the secondary consumer, because it eats caterpillars. | `eco-chain-secondary` |
| Tertiary consumers | Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers. | 3rd consumer eats the 2nd | A consumer that eats secondary consumers is called a tertiary consumer. Here, the sparrowhawk is the tertiary consumer, because it eats blue tits. | `eco-chain-tertiary` |
| Put it together | Every food chain starts with a producer. | count along from the producer | Food chains always start with a producer. Then come the primary, secondary and tertiary consumers. To name each one, count along the arrows from the producer. | `eco-chain-all` |

### B48-06 · choice · `understanding, guided` · question diagram `eco-pondchain-question`
**Q:** Look at the numbered food chain from a pond. Which number is the secondary consumer?
- 0 Number 1 · 1 Number 2 · **2 Number 3 ✓** · 3 Number 4
- Hint: Count along the arrows from the producer.
- Explanation: Number 1 is the producer, and number 2 eats it, so it is the primary consumer. Number 3 eats the primary consumer, so it is the secondary consumer.

### B48-07 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** Grass → rabbit → fox. How many consumers are in this food chain?
- 0 One · **1 Two ✓** · 2 Three
- Hint: Which organisms eat other organisms?
- Explanation: Grass makes its own food, so it is the producer. The rabbit and the fox both eat other organisms, so there are two consumers.

### B48-08 · teach "Predators and prey"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Predators and prey | Predators hunt and kill their prey. | predator eats prey | Foxes hunt and kill rabbits for food. You met predators when you learned about biotic factors. The animals a predator eats are called its prey. | `eco-cycle-meet` |
| More rabbits, more foxes | When prey increase, predators increase. | more food → more predators | In a stable community, each population is limited by the food it has. When there are more rabbits, the foxes have more food. More young foxes survive, so the number of foxes rises. | `eco-cycle-rise` |
| More foxes, fewer rabbits | When predators increase, prey decrease. | more predators → more prey eaten | Now there are lots of foxes, so more rabbits get eaten. The number of rabbits falls. | `eco-cycle-fall` |
| Fewer rabbits, fewer foxes | When prey decrease, predators decrease. | less food → fewer predators | With fewer rabbits, the foxes have less food. Fewer foxes survive, so their number falls. With fewer foxes, more rabbits survive, and the cycle starts again. | `eco-cycle-low` |
| The lag | The predator peaks come after the prey peaks. | prey peak first, predator peak later | The two populations rise and fall in cycles. There are fewer foxes than rabbits, and each fox peak comes a while after a rabbit peak. This delay is called a lag. It happens because foxes take time to breed and raise young. | `eco-cycle-lag` |

### B48-09 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** Why does the number of foxes rise a while after the number of rabbits rises?
- 0 Foxes eat grass as well · 1 Rabbits hunt foxes · 2 Foxes need more light · **3 It takes time for foxes to breed and raise young ✓**
- Hint: What has to happen before there are more foxes?
- Explanation: More rabbits means more food for foxes. But it takes time for foxes to breed and raise young, so their numbers rise later.

### B48-10 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** The number of foxes is at its highest. What is most likely to happen to the rabbits next?
- **0 Their number falls ✓** · 1 Their number rises · 2 Their number stays the same
- Hint: What do lots of foxes do to rabbits?
- Explanation: With lots of foxes, more rabbits are eaten. So the number of rabbits falls.

### B48-11 · choice · `application, independent`
**Q:** In a pond: algae → water flea → stickleback → pike. Which organism is the tertiary consumer?
- 0 Water flea · 1 Stickleback · **2 Pike ✓** · 3 Algae
- Hint: Count along from the producer: primary, secondary, tertiary.
- Explanation: Algae are the producer, the water flea is the primary consumer and the stickleback is the secondary consumer. The pike eats the secondary consumer, so it is the tertiary consumer.

### B48-12 · choice · `dataInterpretation, independent` · question diagram `eco-cycle-question`
**Q:** The graph shows the numbers of voles and of the owls that eat them. Which line shows the owls?
- 0 Line A · **1 Line B ✓** · 2 You cannot tell from the graph
- Hint: Predators are usually fewer, and their peaks come later.
- Explanation: There are usually fewer predators than prey, and predator peaks come after prey peaks. Line B is lower and peaks after line A, so line B shows the owls.

### B48-13 · choice · `dataInterpretation, independent` · question diagram `eco-vole-data`
**Q:** The table shows voles and owls counted in one wood. Which conclusion fits the counts?
- 0 Owls were the only reason vole numbers fell · **1 Owl numbers peaked one year after vole numbers peaked ✓** · 2 There were always more owls than voles · 3 Vole numbers stayed the same
- Hint: Find the year each population was highest.
- Explanation: Voles were highest in year 3, and owls were highest in year 4. So owl numbers peaked one year after vole numbers. The counts alone cannot show that owls were the only cause.

### B48-14 · choice · `application, independent`
**Q:** A disease kills most of the rabbits in an area. What is most likely to happen to the foxes that eat them?
- **0 Their number falls, because they have less food ✓** · 1 Their number rises, because there is less competition · 2 Nothing, because foxes are predators
- Hint: What do the foxes eat?
- Explanation: The rabbits are the foxes’ food. With fewer rabbits, fewer foxes survive, so their number falls.

### B48-15 · written · `explanation, transfer`
**Q:** Explain why the numbers of a predator and its prey rise and fall in cycles. Use foxes and rabbits as your example.
- Hint: Start with more rabbits. Then follow what happens to the foxes, and then back to the rabbits.
- Model answer: When there are more rabbits, the foxes have more food, so more foxes survive and their number rises. More foxes eat more rabbits, so the number of rabbits falls. Then the foxes have less food, so their number falls, and the rabbits can increase again. The fox numbers change a while after the rabbit numbers, because foxes take time to breed.
- Teacher-reviewed (not auto-marked). Rubric points:
  - More rabbits means more food, so the number of foxes rises.
  - More foxes eat more rabbits, so the number of rabbits falls.
  - Fewer rabbits means less food, so the number of foxes falls and the rabbits can increase again.
  - Fox numbers lag behind rabbit numbers, because foxes take time to breed.
- Reject:
  - Saying foxes and rabbits rise and fall at exactly the same time.
  - Saying rabbits eat foxes.
  - Saying there are usually more foxes than rabbits.
