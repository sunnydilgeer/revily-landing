# Chemistry Lesson 50H storyboard — Extracting copper

Chapter C10, Using resources. A whole lesson that only Higher students get (catalogue entry in `higher/lessons.ts`; the badge is shown by the app, so no learner text names the tier). Folder `chemistry/lesson-50h`, id `C-RES-050H-C`, skill `C-EXTRACT`. Visuals: `components/HigherCopperVisuals.tsx` (focus prefix `hcopper-`). It sits after Lesson 50 (reuse and recycling) and before Lesson 51 (life cycle assessments).

Big idea: copper is finite and copper-rich ores are running out, so copper is now taken from low-grade ores. Two biological methods avoid digging, moving and dumping huge amounts of rock: bioleaching (bacteria make the copper compounds in the ore soluble, giving a leachate of copper ions) and phytomining (plants take up copper, which builds up in the leaves; the plants are harvested, dried and burned, and the ash contains soluble copper compounds). Both give a solution of copper ions; electrolysis or displacement with scrap iron gives copper metal. The methods do much less damage to the environment, but they are slow.

Flow note: builds on finite resources and sustainability (Lessons 49–50), electrolysis and displacement without re-teaching them. The reason comes first (why we need low-grade ores, and why mining them the old way is a problem), then the two methods one at a time, each ending at a solution of copper ions, then the shared last step and the trade-off. The visuals reuse the hand-drawn objects and colours of Lessons 49–50 (`ResourceVisuals`, `RecycleVisuals`) and the course's pink bacteria.

1. **Start here** (C50H-01): what "finite" means (prior knowledge).
2. **Copper is running short** (C50H-02–04): three panels built up: copper is finite (wire, pipe) → copper-rich vs low-grade ore → rich ore running out, lots of low-grade left → traditional mining digs, moves and dumps rock → the two new ways. Practice: what a low-grade ore is; why low-grade ores are now used.
3. **Bioleaching** (C50H-05–07): ore heap and a tank: bacteria in solution → ore added → close-up: bacteria make copper compounds soluble → leachate (blue, copper ions) run off → three numbered steps. Practice: what the bacteria do; what the leachate is.
4. **Phytomining** (C50H-08–10): soil with copper specks and three plants → copper builds up in the leaves → harvest and dry → burn in a furnace → ash with soluble copper compounds. Practice: order of the steps; why the plants are burned.
5. **From solution to copper** (C50H-11–14): leachate and dissolved ash → solution of copper ions → electrolysis (copper on the negative electrode) → scrap iron displaces copper → "much less damage, but slow". Worked: scrap iron in a copper sulfate leachate (word equation). Practice: which electrode copper forms at; the main disadvantage (slow).
6. **On your own** (C50H-15–19): numbered phytomining diagram (assessment view: numbers only) — where copper builds up (point 2, the leaves); made-up data for three methods read without over-claiming (no cost data); nickel phytomining (the same method for another metal); silver cannot displace copper; written: describe phytomining and give one advantage and one disadvantage.

Numbers: C50H-16 table (made-up, one imaginary site, 1 tonne of copper from low-grade ore): traditional mining 1 month, 900 t waste rock; bioleaching 2 years, 40 t; phytomining 5 years, 0 t.

Out of scope: the rest of p166 and p167 (life cycle assessments, Lessons 51 and 52); how fast or cheap each method is in real life; the ionic equation for displacement; other metals beyond one transfer question; the book's examples, questions, cartoons and jokes.

Source boundary: supplied revision-guide page 166, top half only ("Copper-Rich Ores are in Short Supply"), scope only; AQA 8464 Chemistry 5.10.1.4 (HT). "Digging, moving and disposing of large amounts of rock" is the spec's wording of why traditional mining is damaging. All examples, numbers, questions, diagrams and wording are original. Judgement calls for the teacher:
- The ash is "dissolved to give a solution of copper ions"; the page only says the ash contains soluble copper compounds and that both methods produce a solution. No solvent (water or acid) is named.
- Electrolysis is described as copper ions gaining electrons at the negative electrode (from the electrolysis lessons), not as a new idea here.
- The worked example names copper sulfate as the copper compound in a leachate and gives the word equation iron + copper sulfate → iron sulfate + copper.
- C50H-17 uses nickel to test transfer, based on the page's note that these methods can extract other metals too.
- C50H-16's data are invented for reading practice; the bioleaching waste-rock figure is not a real value.
