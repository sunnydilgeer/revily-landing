# Lesson 32 storyboard — The rate of photosynthesis

Second lesson of chapter B4, Bioenergetics (folder `lesson-27`, id `B-BIO-027-B`). It follows photosynthesis and what plants do with glucose.

Big idea: photosynthesis can only go as fast as its scarcest supply allows. Whichever of light, carbon dioxide or temperature is in shortest supply (or too little chlorophyll) is the limiting factor, and the pondweed practical is how scientists measure it.

Flow note: one question runs through the whole lesson: *what stops a plant photosynthesising any faster?* It opens with something familiar, a pot plant moved into a dark cupboard, then answers in four steps.
1. **The idea of a limiting factor.** Rate is defined first (how fast glucose is made), then the three factors, then "the one in shortest supply holds it back" with an original sandwich-team analogy. Chlorophyll comes last, because it only makes sense once "limiting factor" exists.
2. **Reading the graphs.** The light graph is taught in two frames (rising part, then flat part) before the carbon dioxide graph reuses the same shape, so learners see the pattern once and then transfer it. A "put it together" frame gives the one reading rule: still rising means the factor on the bottom axis is limiting.
3. **Temperature.** It comes after the graphs because its graph has a different shape (peak, then fall) that is easiest to read against the rise-and-level-off shape just learned. It links back to enzymes (slow when cold; damaged, or denatured, when too hot; stops at about 45 °C).
4. **The pondweed practical.** It comes last, because learners need "rate" and "light intensity" before they can plan a test of them. The method is one apparatus drawing built up step by step, followed by the mean calculation (worked example → practice with new numbers → an independent item in "On your own").

1. Start here (B27-01): a pot plant moved into a dark cupboard (links to the photosynthesis lesson).
2. What holds it back? (B27-02–04): how fast → three things matter (light intensity) → the one in short supply (limiting factor) → chlorophyll. Checks: what a limiting factor is; pale leaves from poor soil.
3. What do the graphs show? (B27-05–07): more light, faster → then it levels off → carbon dioxide, same shape → put it together. Checks: a numbered graph (where light stops limiting); plenty of light and carbon dioxide but a flat rate.
4. Too hot or too cold? (B27-08–10): too cold → too hot → about 45 °C. Checks: a plant at 50 °C; a sunny, frosty morning.
5. Test it with pondweed (B27-11–14): oxygen shows the rate → set the distance → collect the oxygen (capillary tube) → repeat, then move the lamp → keep it fair (control variables, proportionate lamp safety). Worked example: the mean bubble length. Checks: a mean with new numbers; why temperature must stay the same.
6. On your own (B27-15–19): a cold, bright greenhouse; a numbered apparatus diagram; a mean from an original results table; a conclusion from the same table without over-claiming; a teacher-reviewed written method.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; words written out in full; British spelling. The lesson prepares learners for the required practical; it never says they have done it. Safety is kept to the lamp (hot bulb, water away from the plug).

Out of scope: the inverse square law and light intensity calculations (Higher only); interaction of factors and greenhouse economics (Higher only); counting bubbles is mentioned as an alternative only; respiration (next lesson).

## States in full

### B27-01 · choice · `priorKnowledge`, `diagnostic`
**Q:** A potted plant is moved from a sunny windowsill into a dark cupboard. What happens to its photosynthesis?
- 0 It speeds up · **1 It slows down and stops ✓** · 2 It stays the same
- Hint: You learned what photosynthesis needs to work.
- Explanation: Photosynthesis needs energy from light. In the dark there is no light, so photosynthesis slows down and stops.

### B27-02 · teach "What holds it back?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How fast? | The rate of photosynthesis is how fast a plant makes glucose. | rate = how fast | A plant makes glucose faster on some days than on others. How fast photosynthesis happens is called the rate of photosynthesis. When the rate is high, the plant makes more glucose and gives out more oxygen. | `energy-limit-rate` |
| Three things matter | Light, carbon dioxide and temperature all affect the rate. | light intensity = how bright the light is | Photosynthesis needs energy from light. How bright the light is, is called the light intensity. The rate also depends on the concentration of carbon dioxide in the air, and on the temperature. | `energy-limit-factors` |
| The one in short supply | Whatever is in shortest supply holds the rate back. | shortest supply → sets the rate | Think of a team making sandwiches. If they run out of bread, extra cheese will not help them go faster. A plant is the same. Something that stops photosynthesis going any faster is called a limiting factor. Light, carbon dioxide or temperature can each be the limiting factor. | `energy-limit-limiting` |
| Chlorophyll | Too little chlorophyll can also hold the rate back. | less chlorophyll → less light absorbed | Chlorophyll absorbs the light for photosynthesis. Disease, or a lack of nutrients in the soil, can damage chloroplasts or stop the plant making enough chlorophyll. The leaves then absorb less light, so the rate goes down. So the amount of chlorophyll can be a limiting factor too. | `energy-limit-chlorophyll` |

- Hint: Think of the sandwich team that ran out of bread.
- Explanation: A limiting factor is the thing in shortest supply. So it stops photosynthesis going any faster, even when everything else is plentiful.

### B27-04 · choice
**Q:** A plant in soil short of nutrients has pale leaves. Why does its rate of photosynthesis go down?
- 0 Its leaves take in too much carbon dioxide · 1 Pale leaves get too hot · **2 It has less chlorophyll, so it absorbs less light ✓**
- Hint: What does chlorophyll do?
- Explanation: A lack of nutrients stops the plant making enough chlorophyll. With less chlorophyll, the leaves absorb less light, so the rate goes down.

### B27-05 · teach "What do the graphs show?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| More light, faster | At first, more light makes photosynthesis faster. | line going up → light is limiting | This graph shows the rate of photosynthesis as the light gets brighter. At first, the line goes up. More light gives faster photosynthesis. So here, light intensity is the limiting factor. | `energy-graph-rise` |

| Put it together | Is the line still rising? Then that factor is limiting. | still rising → the factor on the bottom axis | To read these graphs, ask whether the line is still going up. ### B27-07 · choice

- **0 Temperature ✓** · 1 Light intensity · 2 Carbon dioxide concentration · 3 Oxygen
- Hint: Which of the three factors is not plentiful?
- Explanation: Light and carbon dioxide are both plentiful, so neither is holding the rate back. So temperature is probably the limiting factor.

### B27-08 · teach "Too hot or too cold?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Too cold | In the cold, photosynthesis is slow. | cold → enzymes work slowly | Photosynthesis needs enzymes, which you met when you learned how enzymes work. Enzymes work slowly when it is cold. So when temperature is the limiting factor, it is usually because it is too low. Warming the plant speeds photosynthesis up. | `energy-temp-cold` |
| Too hot | If the plant gets too hot, its enzymes are damaged. | too hot → enzymes damaged → slower | If the plant gets too hot, the enzymes it needs for photosynthesis are damaged. This is the denaturing you met when you learned about enzymes. So above a certain temperature, the rate falls quickly. | `energy-temp-hot` |
| About 45 °C | Photosynthesis stops at about 45 °C. | rises, peaks, falls to zero | The temperature graph goes up, reaches a peak, then falls. At about 45 °C, photosynthesis stops altogether. So the temperature has to be just right: not too cold and not too hot. | `energy-temp-45` |

### B27-09 · choice
**Q:** A plant is kept at 50 °C. Why does it not photosynthesise?
- 0 It has too much light · **1 The enzymes it needs are damaged by the heat ✓** · 2 Its enzymes work too slowly in the heat
- Hint: What does too much heat do to enzymes?
- Explanation: Above the peak, heat damages the enzymes photosynthesis needs. Photosynthesis stops at about 45 °C, so at 50 °C it does not happen.

### B27-10 · choice
**Q:** It is a sunny but frosty morning. Why is photosynthesis slow?
- 0 There is too much light · **1 It is too cold, so the enzymes work slowly ✓** · 2 The enzymes are damaged by the heat
- Hint: Which factor is in short supply on a frosty morning?
- Explanation: There is plenty of light, but it is very cold. Enzymes work slowly in the cold, so temperature is the limiting factor.

### B27-11 · teach "Test it with pondweed"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxygen shows the rate | Pondweed gives off oxygen bubbles as it photosynthesises. | more oxygen → faster photosynthesis | Pondweed is a plant that lives underwater. As it photosynthesises, it gives off bubbles of oxygen. The faster it makes oxygen, the faster the rate of photosynthesis. This practical uses pondweed to see how light intensity affects the rate, ready for when you do it in class. | `energy-rp-idea` |
| Set the distance | A ruler sets how far the lamp is from the pondweed. | closer lamp → brighter light | The pondweed sits in a tube of water. Use a ruler to put a lamp at a set distance from it. The closer the lamp, the higher the light intensity. Then leave the pondweed to photosynthesise for a set time. | `energy-rp-distance` |
| Collect the oxygen | The oxygen collects in a thin tube. | longer bubble → faster rate | The oxygen collects in a thin glass tube called a capillary tube. At the end, a syringe pulls the gas bubble along the tube, next to a ruler. Measure the length of the bubble. The longer the bubble, the faster the rate of photosynthesis. | `energy-rp-collect` |
| Repeat, then move the lamp | Repeat each distance, then try other distances. | repeat → mean; then change the distance | Repeat the test twice more with the lamp at the same distance, then work out the mean bubble length. After that, repeat the whole test with the lamp at different distances. Another way to measure is to count the bubbles given off in a set time. | `energy-rp-repeat` |
| Keep it fair | Only the lamp distance should change. | change one thing; keep the rest the same | Everything else that could affect the results must stay the same, such as the temperature and the time. These are called control variables. A hot lamp can warm the water, so check the temperature. Take care: lamps get hot, so do not touch the bulb, and keep water away from the plug. | `energy-rp-fair` |

### B27-12 · worked example "Work out a mean bubble length" · visual `energy-rp-mean`
Diagram: a results row for the lamp at 10 cm (1.2, 1.5, 1.2 cm, mean shown as ?), the add-then-divide working, and three bars drawn to scale with a dashed line at the 1.3 cm mean.

**Problem:** With the lamp 10 cm away, three tests gave bubble lengths of 1.2 cm, 1.5 cm and 1.2 cm. What is the mean bubble length?
1. Add the three lengths: 1.2 + 1.5 + 1.2 = 3.9 cm.
2. Divide by the number of tests: 3.9 ÷ 3 = 1.3.
3. Add the units: the mean bubble length is 1.3 cm.

### B27-14 · choice · `practicalReasoning`

**Q:** Which conclusion fits these results?
- **0 In this test, the closer the lamp, the longer the bubble, so the faster the rate ✓** · 1 Pondweed stops photosynthesising when the lamp is more than 40 cm away · 2 Light is the only thing that affects photosynthesis · 3 The lamp at 10 cm heated the water
- Hint: Only say what these results show.
- Explanation: The mean bubble length fell from 1.3 cm at 10 cm to 0.3 cm at 40 cm. So in this test, the closer the lamp, the faster the rate. The results say nothing about distances beyond 40 cm or other factors.

### B27-19 · written · `teacherOnly`
**Q:** Describe how you could use pondweed to find out how light intensity affects the rate of photosynthesis.
- Hint: Say what you change, what you measure, how you make it reliable and what you keep the same.
- Model answer: Put pondweed in a tube of water and use a ruler to place a lamp a set distance away. Leave it for a set time and collect the oxygen in a capillary tube, then measure the length of the gas bubble. Repeat twice more at that distance and work out the mean. Then repeat with the lamp at different distances, keeping the temperature and the time the same.
- Marking points: A ruler is used to put the lamp a set distance from the pondweed. · The pondweed is left to photosynthesise for a set time. · The oxygen is collected and the length of the gas bubble is measured (or the bubbles are counted). · Each distance is repeated and a mean is worked out. · The test is repeated with the lamp at different distances. · The temperature and the time are kept the same.
- Common errors: Saying the pondweed gives off carbon dioxide, or measuring carbon dioxide. · Changing the lamp distance and the temperature at the same time. · Saying a longer bubble means a slower rate.
