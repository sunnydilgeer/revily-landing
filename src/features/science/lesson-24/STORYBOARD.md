# Lesson 24 storyboard — Medicines and where they come from (v2)

Big idea: some medicines only ease symptoms, while antibiotics kill bacteria (but not viruses, and not resistant strains); many medicines were first found in plants and microorganisms.

Flow note: the lesson follows one pupil, Mia, through two illnesses. It opens with something familiar: Mia has flu and takes a painkiller, which eases her headache but does nothing to the virus. Her white blood cells clear it (a brief link to Lesson 22). A month later, she has an ear infection caused by bacteria, and the doctor gives her an antibiotic. The same story then explains why she was not given one for flu. Painkillers come before antibiotics because "symptom" has to be understood before "cure". Viruses come after antibiotics because "antibiotics do not kill viruses" only makes sense once you know what antibiotics do. Resistance follows naturally: the doctor chose the right antibiotic, but some strains resist every usual choice. The lesson ends with Mia's medicine cupboard, tracing where the medicines came from: willow, foxglove, mould, then the lab.

1. Start here (B24-01): which type of pathogen reproduces inside body cells? (links to Lesson 19).
2. Mia's flu (B24-02–03): flu → symptoms → painkillers → her white blood cells clear it. Check: what the painkiller did to the virus.
3. The ear infection (B24-04–06): bacteria → antibiotics → the right one → not for viruses → painkillers and antibiotics side by side, with fewer deaths. Checks: numbered zoom diagram (which pathogen an antibiotic could kill); why flu is not treated with antibiotics.
4. When antibiotics stop working (B24-07–08): mutate → resistant (links to Lesson 20) → resistant strains and MRSA. Check: what resistant means.
5. Where Mia's medicines came from (B24-09–11): plant chemicals → aspirin from willow → digitalis from foxgloves → penicillin from Fleming's mould → the pharmaceutical industry. Checks: numbered sources diagram (penicillin); which drug came from willow.
6. On your own (B24-12–16): her brother's cold; why antiviral drugs are hard to make; an original clear-zone chart read without over-claiming; why the right antibiotic matters; teacher-reviewed written explanation.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; words written out in full; British spelling. Neutral, factual tone about medicines: no advice to take or avoid any medicine, and no absolute claims ("most", "usually", not "always" or "never"). Nothing here is personal medical advice.

Out of scope: how resistance spreads by natural selection (a later lesson; forward link only); testing new drugs, including efficacy, toxicity, dose, trials, placebos and peer review (Lesson 25); named antiviral drugs; monoclonal antibodies and plant defences (Biology only); doses and brand names.

Source boundary: supplied page 42 (reference only); AQA 8464 sections 4.3.1.8 and the first part of 4.3.1.9. All scenarios, questions, the Mia story and the chart are original. Draft pending teacher review.

## States in full

### B24-01 · choice · `priorKnowledge`, `diagnostic`
**Q:** Which type of pathogen reproduces inside your body cells?
- 0 Bacteria · **1 Viruses ✓** · 2 Fungi
- Hint: You met the types of pathogen in Lesson 19.
- Explanation: Bacteria and fungi are cells, and they can reproduce outside your body cells. Viruses are not cells, and they reproduce inside your body cells.

### B24-12 · choice · `application`, independent
**Q:** Mia's brother Tom has a cold, caused by a virus. He asks for antibiotics. What would a doctor most likely suggest?

### B24-13 · choice · `understanding`, independent
**Q:** Why is it hard to develop drugs that destroy viruses?
- 0 Viruses are too big for drugs to reach · 1 Viruses have no antigens · 2 Viruses never cause serious disease · **3 Viruses reproduce inside body cells, so a drug could damage the cells too ✓**

### B24-15 · choice · `understanding`, independent
**Q:** Why is it important to be treated with the right antibiotic?
- **0 Different antibiotics kill different types of bacteria ✓** · 1 Some antibiotics kill viruses instead of bacteria · 2 The wrong antibiotic turns bacteria into viruses
- Hint: Think about Mia's ear infection.
- Explanation: Antibiotics do not all work on the same bacteria. Different antibiotics kill different types of bacteria.

### B24-16 · written · `teacherOnly`
**Q:** Mia was given an antibiotic for her ear infection, but not for her flu. Explain why.
- Hint: Name the type of pathogen behind each illness, then say what antibiotics do.
- Model answer: Mia's ear infection was caused by bacteria. Antibiotics kill bacteria inside the body, so the antibiotic could clear the infection. Her flu was caused by a virus. Antibiotics do not kill viruses, so an antibiotic would not have helped; a painkiller could ease her symptoms instead.
- Marking points: The ear infection was caused by bacteria. · Antibiotics kill bacteria. · Flu is caused by a virus. · Antibiotics do not kill viruses.
- Common errors: Saying antibiotics kill all pathogens. · Saying painkillers kill pathogens. · Saying the flu virus was "resistant" rather than that antibiotics do not work on viruses.
