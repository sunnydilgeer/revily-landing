# Lesson 10 storyboard — The lungs

Big idea: follow one breath. Air goes in through the nose or mouth, down the trachea, into the bronchi and on to the alveoli. There, oxygen diffuses into the blood and carbon dioxide diffuses back out. Four features (large area, thin walls, good blood supply, ventilation) make this fast.

Anchor / route: nose or mouth → trachea → bronchi → smaller tubes → alveoli → oxygen into the blood; carbon dioxide from the blood into the alveolus → breathed out.

Why this order:
- The route comes first, so "alveolus" is a place learners have already reached before anything happens there.
- Why airways branch is taught in the route walkthrough (B10-02 frame 3) before B10-04 asks it.
- Gas exchange (what happens) comes before the adaptations (why it is fast), so each adaptation has something to speed up.
- Blood supply is taught before ventilation, so "keep the difference big" is met once for blood and then reused for air.
- Ventilation is defined (B10-11 frame 1) before B10-12, B10-13 and the written mark scheme use it.
- Lesson 10 is now the home of why alveoli work well. It is written fresh rather than repeating Lesson 6's exchange-surface frame.

Hand-offs:
- Back to Lesson 9: "In Lesson 9, digested food passed into your blood. Your cells also need oxygen…" (B10-02 frame 1). Diffusion is linked back to Lesson 6 in one clause.
- On to Lesson 11: "Then the oxygen-rich blood goes to the heart next, which you will follow in the next lesson." (B10-11 final frame).

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first, then "is called …". Bronchioles are named once in B10-02 as supporting context; they are never assessed. The route question is asked once (B10-03); "On your own" tests the route through a spot-the-error item instead.

Out of scope: breathing-rate arithmetic, the mechanism of breathing (ribs, diaphragm, pressure), naming lung diseases, cartilage rings, mucus and cilia.
