# Lesson 34 storyboard — Exercise and metabolism

Fourth lesson of chapter B4, Bioenergetics (folder `lesson-29`, id `B-BIO-029-B`). It follows aerobic and anaerobic respiration.

Big idea: exercising muscles need more energy, so the body delivers more oxygen by breathing faster and deeper and by raising heart rate; when oxygen cannot keep up, muscles respire anaerobically and the body pays back an oxygen debt afterwards. Respiration is one of many reactions in the body, and all of them together are metabolism.

Flow note: the lesson follows one run from start to finish, then zooms out.
1. **Why breathe harder?** Muscles first (they contract, so they need energy and oxygen), because every other change exists to supply them. Then breathing rate, breath volume and heart rate, one term per frame, on one body drawing with a numbered step list, ending with a "put it together" frame. The fitness side note sits on the heart rate frame, ready for the heart rate data in "On your own".
2. **Really hard exercise.** Anaerobic respiration is recapped from the last lesson in one clause, then lactic acid and muscle fatigue, shown in a zoomed-in thigh muscle.
3. **Why keep panting?** After the run: still breathing hard, the oxygen debt (the extra oxygen needed after exercise), and paying it back from lungs to blood to muscles, on one breathing-rate graph.
4. **All the reactions.** Metabolism comes last, because it pulls together respiration (breaking down) and the building-up reactions learners met in the photosynthesis lesson. The name "metabolism" comes in the final frame, after the examples.

1. Start here (B29-01): running for a bus.
2. Why breathe harder? (B29-02–04): muscles need energy → breathe faster (breathing rate) → breathe deeper (breath volume) → heart beats faster (heart rate) → put it together. Checks: why breathing rate increases; the chain in the right order.
3. Really hard exercise (B29-05–07): not enough oxygen → lactic acid builds up → muscle fatigue. Checks: why a sprinter's legs hurt; what muscle fatigue is.
4. Why keep panting? (B29-08–10): after you stop → oxygen debt → paying it back. Checks: what an oxygen debt is; why a swimmer keeps breathing hard.
5. All the reactions (B29-11–13): lots of reactions (enzymes) → building from glucose (glycogen) → lipids → proteins → breaking down (urea) → metabolism. Checks: the best description of metabolism; which reaction builds a larger molecule.
6. On your own (B29-14–17): an original two-student heart rate graph read without over-claiming; a numbered body diagram (where lactic acid builds up); extra protein; a teacher-reviewed written answer about a cyclist on a hill.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; British spelling. Health and fitness are described neutrally; the data question says what the graph shows, not that anyone is unfit or ill.

Out of scope: lactic acid carried to the liver and converted back to glucose, and oxygen debt defined in terms of lactic acid (Higher only); the word "excreted" (urea is said to leave the body in urine); the details of enzymes (the enzymes lesson).

### B29-01 · choice · `priorKnowledge`, `diagnostic`
**Q:** You run for a bus. What do you notice straight afterwards?
- 0 You breathe more slowly than usual · **1 You breathe faster and your heart beats faster ✓** · 2 Your heart stops beating for a moment
- Hint: Think about how you feel after running.
- Explanation: After running, you breathe fast and deeply. Your heart beats faster too.

### B29-02 · teach "Why breathe harder?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Muscles need energy | Muscles need energy from respiration to contract. | more contracting → more respiration → more oxygen | Muscles move you by getting shorter, which is called contracting. The energy comes from respiration in the muscle cells. During exercise, muscles contract more often, so they need more energy. More respiration needs more oxygen. | `energy-exercise-muscle` |
| Breathe faster | Your breathing rate goes up. | breathing rate = how fast you breathe | How fast you breathe is called your breathing rate. During exercise, your breathing rate increases. More air reaches your lungs each minute. | `energy-exercise-rate` |
| Breathe deeper | Your breath volume goes up. | breath volume = how deep each breath is | How deep each breath is, is called your breath volume. During exercise, your breath volume increases too. Together with faster breathing, this gets more oxygen into your blood. | `energy-exercise-volume` |
| Heart beats faster | Your heart rate goes up. | heart rate = how fast your heart beats | How fast your heart beats is called your heart rate. During exercise, your heart rate increases. This carries oxygenated blood to the muscles faster, as you saw when you learned about the heart. In a less fit person, heart rate usually rises more and takes longer to return to normal. | `energy-exercise-heart` |
| Put it together | Four changes get more oxygen to working muscles. | muscles → lungs → heart | Muscles contract more, so they respire more and need more oxygen. Breathing rate and breath volume increase to get more oxygen into the blood. Heart rate increases to carry that blood to the muscles faster. | `energy-exercise-all` |

### B29-03 · choice
**Q:** Why does your breathing rate increase when you exercise?
- 0 To breathe out lactic acid · 1 To cool the lungs down · **2 To get more oxygen into the blood for respiration in the muscles ✓**
- Hint: What do working muscles need more of?
- Explanation: Working muscles respire more, so they need more oxygen. Faster breathing gets more oxygen into the blood.

### B29-04 · choice
**Q:** Which chain is in the right order?
- **0 Muscles contract more → more respiration → more oxygen needed → faster breathing and heart rate ✓** · 1 Faster heart rate → less respiration → muscles contract less · 2 More oxygen needed → muscles contract less → slower breathing
- Hint: Start with what the muscles are doing.
- Explanation: Exercise makes muscles contract more, so they respire more and need more oxygen. Breathing rate, breath volume and heart rate all increase to deliver it.

### B29-05 · teach "Really hard exercise"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Not enough oxygen | In really hard exercise, oxygen cannot arrive fast enough. | not enough oxygen → anaerobic as well | In really hard exercise, your body cannot supply oxygen to your muscles quickly enough. So the muscles start respiring anaerobically as well, as you saw when you learned about the two kinds of respiration. This is not the best way to transfer energy from glucose. | `energy-hard-short` |
| Lactic acid builds up | Lactic acid builds up in the muscles, and it hurts. | anaerobic → lactic acid → painful | Anaerobic respiration in muscles makes lactic acid. During hard exercise, lactic acid builds up in the muscles. This can be painful. | `energy-hard-lactic` |
| Muscle fatigue | Long periods of exercise make muscles tired. | fatigue = tired, not contracting well | After long periods of exercise, muscles get tired and stop contracting efficiently. This is called muscle fatigue. | `energy-hard-fatigue` |

### B29-06 · choice
**Q:** Near the end of a hard sprint, a runner’s legs hurt. What is the most likely cause?
- 0 Too much oxygen in the leg muscles · 1 The heart beating too slowly · **2 Lactic acid building up in the leg muscles ✓**
- Hint: What does anaerobic respiration make in muscles?
- Explanation: In a hard sprint, not enough oxygen reaches the muscles, so they respire anaerobically as well. This makes lactic acid, which builds up and is painful.

### B29-07 · choice
**Q:** What is muscle fatigue?
- 0 Muscles growing bigger after exercise · **1 Muscles getting tired and not contracting efficiently ✓** · 2 Muscles making their own oxygen
- Hint: Fatigue means tiredness.
- Explanation: Long periods of exercise make muscles tired. Tired muscles stop contracting efficiently. This is muscle fatigue.

### B29-08 · teach "Why keep panting?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| After you stop | You keep breathing hard after exercise. | stopping running ≠ stopping breathing hard | When you stop exercising, your breathing does not go straight back to normal. You keep breathing hard for a while. The graph shows breathing rate before, during and after a run. | `energy-debt-after` |
| Oxygen debt | The extra oxygen you need after exercise is an oxygen debt. | debt = oxygen your muscles are still owed | During hard exercise, your lungs, heart and blood could not keep up with the muscles’ need for oxygen. | Paying it back | Breathing hard gets oxygen to the muscle cells. | lungs → blood → muscle cells | Breathing hard after exercise gets more oxygen into your blood. The blood carries it to the muscle cells. Your breathing slowly returns to normal as the debt is paid back. | `energy-debt-repay` |

### B29-09 · choice
**Q:** What is an oxygen debt?
- **0 The amount of extra oxygen your body needs after exercise ✓** · 1 The oxygen you breathe out after exercise · 2 The oxygen stored in your muscles
- Hint: A debt is something you still owe.
- Explanation: During hard exercise, the lungs, heart and blood cannot keep up with the muscles. The extra oxygen the body needs afterwards is the oxygen debt.

### B29-10 · choice
**Q:** Why does a swimmer keep breathing hard after a race?
- **0 To get extra oxygen into the blood for the muscle cells ✓** · 1 To make more lactic acid · 2 Because the lungs have stopped working
- Hint: What does the body still need after hard exercise?
- Explanation: The swimmer has an oxygen debt after the race. Breathing hard gets extra oxygen into the blood, which carries it to the muscle cells.

### B29-11 · teach "All the reactions"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lots of reactions | Cells carry out many reactions all the time. | reactions → controlled by enzymes | In every cell, lots of chemical reactions happen all the time. Enzymes control these reactions, as you saw when you learned about enzymes. Some reactions build larger molecules, and others break molecules down. | `energy-meta-reactions` |
| Building from glucose | Glucose is joined up to make larger molecules. | many glucose → starch, glycogen, cellulose | Many small glucose molecules can be joined together. Plants make starch to store and cellulose for their cell walls. Animals join glucose into a storage molecule called glycogen. | `energy-meta-build` |
| Lipids | A lipid is made from glycerol and three fatty acids. | 1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids | Lipids are fats and oils. Each lipid molecule is made from one molecule of glycerol joined to three fatty acids. | `energy-meta-lipid` |
| Proteins | Amino acids join to make proteins. | glucose + nitrate → amino acids → protein | Glucose is combined with nitrate ions to make amino acids, as you saw when you learned what plants do with glucose. Amino acids are then joined together to make proteins. | `energy-meta-protein` |

| Metabolism | All the reactions together are your metabolism. | metabolism = the sum of all the reactions | Now add up every reaction in a cell or in the body, building up and breaking down. The sum of all these reactions is called metabolism. | `energy-meta-all` |

### B29-12 · choice
**Q:** Which is the best description of metabolism?
- 0 Only the reactions that break down food · **1 The sum of all the reactions in a cell or the body ✓** · 2 How fast you breathe · 3 The energy stored in fat
- Hint: It covers building up and breaking down.
- Explanation: Cells build some molecules up and break others down, all controlled by enzymes. The sum of all these reactions is metabolism.

### B29-13 · choice
**Q:** Which reaction builds a larger molecule from smaller ones?
- 0 Glucose → carbon dioxide + water · 1 Extra protein → urea · **2 Glucose → glycogen ✓**
- Hint: Which one joins small molecules together?
- Explanation: Respiration and making urea break molecules down. Joining many glucose molecules makes glycogen, a larger molecule.

### B29-17 · written · `teacherOnly`
**Q:** A cyclist rides hard up a long, steep hill. Explain how her body gets more oxygen to her leg muscles, and what happens if it cannot get enough.
- Hint: Start with what the leg muscles need, then her lungs and heart, then what happens without enough oxygen.
- Model answer: Her leg muscles contract more, so they respire more and need more oxygen. Her breathing rate and breath volume increase, so more oxygen gets into her blood. Her heart rate increases, so oxygenated blood reaches the muscles faster. If not enough oxygen arrives, the muscles also respire anaerobically, and lactic acid builds up. Afterwards she keeps breathing hard to repay the oxygen debt.
- Marking points: Muscles contract more, so they need more energy from respiration and more oxygen. · Breathing rate and breath volume increase, so more oxygen gets into the blood. · Heart rate increases, so oxygenated blood reaches the muscles faster. · Without enough oxygen, the muscles also respire anaerobically and lactic acid builds up (painful, or leads to fatigue). · Afterwards there is an oxygen debt, so she keeps breathing hard.
- Common errors: Saying the heart or lungs make oxygen. · Saying muscles breathe, or that respiration is breathing. · Saying anaerobic respiration makes ethanol or more energy.
