# Lesson 47 storyboard — Evolution, speciation and extinction (folder lesson-42)

Second lesson of chapter B6b, Variation, evolution and classification.

Big idea: in a varied population, individuals better adapted to their environment are more likely to survive and pass on their genes, so useful characteristics become common and species change over time (evolution); populations can change so much they become new species, and species can die out altogether.

Flow note: one population of leaf beetles carries the whole lesson. It opens with an everyday puzzle (why are animals in snowy places often white?) and the written task closes on the same idea with hares.
1. **How do species change?** Natural selection one step per frame on one drawing of three leaves (generation 1, the next generation, many generations later): variation → better adapted (birds spot brown beetles) → survivors pass on genes → natural selection → evolution. Each step needs the one before, so the order is fixed.
2. **What is the evidence?** Only once the mechanism is clear does the lesson ask "how do we know?": the theory of evolution (simple life forms over three billion years ago), the gap Darwin could not fill (how characteristics appear and are passed on), then genes, the fossil record and bacteria evolving resistance, so the theory is now widely accepted. Fossil formation and resistance each get their own later lesson; here they are evidence only.
3. **How do new species form?** Two beetle populations in a forest and a desert change so much they cannot breed to produce fertile offspring: speciation.
4. **How do species die out?** Extinction, then its five causes from the page, grouped as too fast (habitat destroyed, catastrophic event), new predator or disease, and losing the competition for food, with a put-it-together frame.

1. Start here (B42-01): why animals in snowy places are often white (diagnostic of natural-selection intuition vs "they changed to fit").
2. How do species change? (B42-02–04): checks why green beetles pass on genes; what happens to their number.
3. What is the evidence? (B42-05–07): checks what Darwin could not explain; which is evidence for evolution.
4. How do new species form? (B42-08–09): check when two populations are separate species.
5. How do species die out? (B42-10–12): checks what extinct means; a native snail and a new competitor.
6. On your own (B42-13–17): rabbits in colder winters (application; Lamarckian distractor); five numbered causes, which is catastrophic (assessment view); an invented graph of green beetles over 30 generations read without over-claiming; how to test whether two fish populations are separate species; teacher-reviewed written answer on white hares.

Wording rules: one new term per frame, plain meaning first; British spelling; Darwin named once as the scientist who explained natural selection.

Out of scope: isolation mechanisms and the word "isolation" (the forest and desert are only a setting); Wallace; how fossils form and the limits of the fossil record (Lesson 50); how antibiotic resistance evolves in detail (Lesson 48); named extinct species; mass extinctions.

Source boundary: supplied page 70 used for scope only; AQA 8464 sections 4.6.2.2, 4.6.3.1 and 4.6.3.3. The beetle population, the forest and desert, the snails, rabbits, fish, hares, the beetle graph and all questions are original. Draft pending teacher review.

## States in full

### B42-01 · choice · `understanding, priorKnowledge, diagnostic`
**Q:** Many animals that live in snowy places have white fur or feathers. Which is the best explanation?
- 0 The cold bleaches their fur or feathers white · **1 White ones were harder to spot, so more of them survived and had young ✓** · 2 Each animal chose to turn white · 3 Rolling in snow turns them white
- Hint: Think about which animals are more likely to be seen by a predator.
- Explanation: An animal cannot bleach or choose its colour, and snow does not change it. White animals were harder to spot, so more survived and had young like them.

### B42-02 · teach "How do species change?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Variation | Beetles in one population vary in colour. | different genes → different colours | A population of beetles lives on green leaves. Their colour varies from green to brown, because of their genes. Birds hunt the beetles. | `evolve-ns-vary` |
| Better adapted | Beetles that suit their environment are more likely to survive. | hard to spot → more likely to survive | Organisms compete for resources, such as food, and must avoid being eaten. Brown beetles are easy for birds to spot on green leaves. Green beetles suit their environment better. We say they are better adapted, so they are more likely to survive. | `evolve-ns-survive` |
| Passing on genes | Survivors reproduce and pass on their genes. | survive → reproduce → genes passed on | The beetles that survive are more likely to reproduce. So the genes for green colour are more likely to be passed on to their offspring. The next generation has more green beetles. | `evolve-ns-breed` |
| Natural selection | Over many generations, the useful characteristic becomes common. | nature picks who survives | This happens again in every generation. Over time, the genes for green colour become more and more common. The environment, not people, decides which individuals survive, so this is called natural selection. | `evolve-ns-select` |
| Evolution | The species changes over time. | useful characteristics become common → the species changes | When useful characteristics become common, the species changes. A species changing over time like this is called evolution. Charles Darwin was the scientist who first explained evolution by natural selection. | `evolve-ns-evolve` |

### B42-03 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** Why are green beetles more likely than brown beetles to pass on their genes?
- **0 They are more likely to survive and reproduce ✓** · 1 They choose to have more offspring · 2 Their genes change to protect them
- Hint: Which beetles do the birds find more easily?
- Explanation: Birds spot brown beetles more easily on green leaves, so green beetles are more likely to survive. Survivors are more likely to reproduce and pass on their genes.

### B42-04 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** What happens to the number of green beetles in the population over many generations?
- 0 It stays the same · 1 It falls · **2 It rises ✓** · 3 It rises, then every beetle becomes brown
- Hint: Whose genes are passed on in each generation?
- Explanation: In each generation more green beetles survive to reproduce. So green beetles become more common: their number rises.

|---|---|---|---|---|

### B42-06 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** What could Darwin not explain in his time?
- 0 Why organisms compete for food · 1 That species change over time · 2 That some organisms survive better than others · **3 How characteristics are passed on to offspring ✓**
- Hint: What were scientists yet to discover in Darwin’s time?
- Explanation: Darwin explained survival and change by natural selection. Nobody then knew about genes, so he could not explain how characteristics are passed on.

### B42-07 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** Which of these is evidence that supports evolution by natural selection?
- **0 Bacteria becoming resistant to antibiotics ✓** · 1 Plants growing towards light · 2 People getting taller when they eat well
- Hint: Which one is a change in a whole population, passed on in genes?
- Explanation: Growing towards light and eating well are changes during one life, not passed on in genes. Bacteria evolving resistance to antibiotics is evidence for evolution.

### B42-08 · teach "How do new species form?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Big changes | Natural selection can change a population a lot. | different places → different characteristics | Imagine two populations of the beetle living in very different places, a green forest and a sandy desert. Natural selection favours different characteristics in each. Over a long time, their phenotypes become very different. | `evolve-spec-split` |
| Cannot breed | They can no longer breed to have fertile offspring. | fertile = can reproduce | In the end, the two populations are so different that they cannot breed with each other to produce fertile offspring. Fertile offspring are young that can themselves reproduce. | `evolve-spec-cannot` |
| Speciation | Two new species have formed. | speciation = new species forming | The two populations are now two different species. When new species form like this, it is called speciation. | `evolve-spec-new` |

### B42-09 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** When have two populations become two separate species?
- 0 When they live in different places · **1 When they can no longer breed together to produce fertile offspring ✓** · 2 When they are different colours · 3 When one population is bigger
- Hint: Think about what the two populations can no longer do.
- Explanation: Living apart or looking different does not by itself make separate species. They are separate species when they cannot breed together to produce fertile offspring.

### B42-10 · teach "How do species die out?"
| Label | Summary | Think: | Text | Focus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Extinction | A species is extinct when no individuals are left. | extinct = none left | Sometimes every individual of a species dies. When no individuals of a species are left, the species is extinct. This is called extinction. | `evolve-ext-none` |
| Too fast | The environment can change faster than the species can adapt. | fast change or disaster | Natural selection is slow. If the environment changes too quickly, such as when a habitat is destroyed, a species may not survive. A catastrophic event, a sudden disaster such as a volcanic eruption, can also kill them all. | `evolve-ext-fast` |
| Predators and disease | A new predator or disease can kill them all. | new enemy → none left | A new predator can kill every individual of a species, for example when humans hunt it. A new disease can also spread through a species and kill all of them. | `evolve-ext-predator` |
| Losing out on food | A species may not win the competition for food. | cannot compete → dies out | Sometimes a new species arrives that eats the same food. If the original species cannot compete with it for food, it may become extinct. | `evolve-ext-food` |
| Put it together | Five reasons a species can become extinct. | too fast, disaster, predator, disease, food | A species can become extinct if its environment changes too quickly, or a catastrophic event happens. It can also happen because of a new predator, a new disease, or a new species it cannot compete with for food. | `evolve-ext-all` |

### B42-12 · choice · `understanding, guided`
**Q:** A new species of snail arrives on an island and eats the same plants as a native snail. Why might the native snail become extinct?

- Hint: What do the two snails both need?

### B42-17 · written · `teacherOnly`
**Q:** Hares in a snowy region have white fur in winter. Explain how natural selection could have led to this.
- Hint: Start with variation, then say which hares survive, then what is passed on, over many generations.
- Model answer: In the past, the hares varied in fur colour because of their genes. White hares were harder for predators to spot in the snow, so they were better adapted and more likely to survive. They were more likely to reproduce and pass on the genes for white fur to their offspring. Over many generations, white fur became more common in the population.
- Marking points: The hares varied in fur colour because of their genes. · White hares were better adapted: harder for predators to spot in the snow. · White hares were more likely to survive and reproduce. · The genes for white fur were passed on to offspring. · Over many generations, white fur became more common.
- Reject: Saying the hares turned white because they needed to, or chose to. · Saying the cold or snow changed the hares’ fur colour, and this was passed on. · Saying each hare evolved during its own life. · Leaving out that genes are passed on to offspring.
